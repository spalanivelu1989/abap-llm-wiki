Writing the Constitution

What the vault's rulebook really is, the requirements to gather from the ABAP team before finalizing it, and rules for writing rules.

What a constitution actually is

Concretely, CLAUDE.md is the instruction file the pipeline pastes into every Claude prompt before Claude reads a new document. It tells Claude what knowledge to extract, how to name pages, which folder each page type belongs in, and how pages must link together. It is the only guidance Claude receives — nobody reviews the output before it lands in the vault, so whatever this file says (or fails to say) directly becomes the shape and quality of the wiki.

That's why it helps to think of it as a contract between three parties, each of whom depends on it in a different way:

  • The team drops files in and reads pages out. The constitution is their guarantee that pages will be named predictably, filed where they expect, and worth trusting.
  • The pipeline runs automatically with no human watching. It can't ask clarifying questions — it can only follow what's written.
  • The AI makes every judgment call alone: is this a decision or an open question? Is "O2C" the same as "OTC"? Does this deserve a new page or an update to an existing one? The constitution is the only thing standing in for a human reviewer.

This leads to a simple litmus test for every rule you write: "When the AI hits an ambiguous situation at 2 AM with nobody watching, does this rule tell it exactly what to do?"

  • Fails the test: "Keep notes high quality." Two runs of the pipeline can interpret this differently, and you can't look at a page and say whether the rule was followed.
  • Passes the test: "If you cannot link a new page upward to a parent page, do not create it — append the content to an existing page instead." Given any input, there is exactly one correct behavior, and you can verify it by looking at the output.

In short: the constitution is not a mission statement about good documentation. It is a decision procedure — a set of "if this, then do that" rules precise enough that an unattended AI produces the same clean, consistent vault no matter who dropped the file or what it looked like.

Requirements to gather from the ABAP team

1. The entity universe (highest priority — dedup depends on it)

In plain terms: an "entity" is anything the team talks about that could show up under more than one name — a workstream, a system, a vendor, a person. The "entity universe" is the complete list of those things, together with every nickname, abbreviation, and misspelling people actually use for them.

Why it matters: when the AI reads a document, it looks up every name it encounters in meta/entities.md (the name dictionary) and converts it to the one official spelling before creating or linking pages — that's the "harmonization check." If the dictionary knows that "O2C", "OTC", and "Order-to-Cash" are all the same workstream, everything files onto one page. If it doesn't, the AI has no way to tell they're the same thing, and you get two or three duplicate pages for one topic — which is why deduplication depends entirely on this list, and why it's the highest-priority thing to collect. The dictionary is only as good as what the team tells you, so gather from them, exhaustively:

  • Every workstream name and every alias people actually use in emails, transcripts, and file names (O2C vs OTC vs "Order-to-Cash stream")
  • SAP module slugs in scope (SD, MM, FI, CO, PP…)
  • System SIDs and their nicknames ("the quality box", "Q-system", QAS)
  • Vendor/partner/client names with spelling variants
  • Key people and how their names get mangled in transcripts (auto-transcription butchers names — get a roster)

2. What actually flows through the pipeline

Ask for real samples of every document type they produce: meeting transcripts, FS/TS documents, WRICEF lists, estimation spreadsheets, code exports, defect logs, email chains. Your format table is generic until you've seen their actual artifacts. A CR document like the FTDS one you already ingested tells you more than any interview.

3. Consumption patterns (this shapes everything)

The vault's structure should be derived from the questions the team actually asks, not from a taxonomy that looks tidy. Gather: What do you look up repeatedly? What did you ask a colleague last week? What would you have wanted on day one of joining this project? What breaks when someone leaves?

4. Existing standards and their real status

Which coding/naming standards exist on paper vs. are actually followed? Ingesting a dead standard as evergreen poisons the vault's credibility.

5. Effort and estimation culture

Your calibration loop (estimates + actuals + variance) is one of the highest-value features, but only if actuals get recorded. Who tracks effort today, in what tool, at what granularity? If the answer is "nobody," you need a lighter rule or it will silently fail.

6. Confidentiality and data boundaries — currently missing from your draft

This is the biggest gap in v1.0. Ask: Can client names appear in the vault? Salary/rate info in estimation docs? PII in transcripts (who said what)? Is there content that must never be committed to GitHub? You likely need a "do not ingest / redact on ingest" rule, and the team must define it — you can't.

7. Governance and trust

Who is the Curator (your draft says TBD — resolve this before go-live, not after)? What's the SLA for SME review of ai-generated pages? What happens when the AI writes something wrong and a client sees it? Who arbitrates when two workstreams record conflicting decisions?

Questions to ask, by audience

Developers:

  • "What did you have to reverse-engineer from code because it wasn't written down? What would have saved you?"
  • "What non-obvious SAP behavior cost you more than a day?" (seeds your gotchas)
  • "When you finish an object, what's the minimum you'd actually write down — honestly?"

Team/workstream leads:

  • "Walk me through your last handover. What was missing?"
  • "What question do junior team members ask you repeatedly?" (seeds FAQs)
  • "How do you estimate today, and would you trust past actuals from this vault to quote future work?"

Functional consultants / client-facing people:

  • "What decisions get relitigated because nobody wrote down the rationale?"
  • "What client questions come back every phase?" (testing, transports, access — your FAQ topics)

Everyone:

  • "When you need to know something today, where do you look first, and where do you actually find it?" (the delta is your value proposition)
  • "What would make you stop trusting this wiki after one bad experience?" (this defines your quality bar concretely)

How to run the gathering process

  1. Shadow before you interview. Sit in one design review and one defect triage. Watch what knowledge gets created and lost in real time — you'll find requirements nobody would articulate.
  2. Pilot ingest with real documents, before finalizing. Take 5–10 real artifacts, run them through the pipeline, and review the output with the people who wrote the originals. Their corrections are your requirements. "The AI put this in Decisions but it's really an open question" → that's a triage rule you were missing.
  3. Run one 60–90 min workshop, not a survey. Agenda: 15 min walking through one ingested page (make it concrete), 30 min on entity naming (project the entities.md draft, argue aliases live), 20 min on the confidentiality boundary, 15 min assigning the Curator and Intelligence Lead by name. Surveys about hypothetical knowledge bases get hypothetical answers.
  4. Establish a feedback channel with near-zero friction. A rule like "if a page is wrong, fix it in Obsidian" only works if people know it's allowed and takes seconds. Ask the team what channel they'd actually use to flag "the AI got this wrong."

Rules for writing the constitution itself

  • Every rule must be enforceable or observable. If you can't tell whether the rule was followed by looking at the output, rewrite it as something you can check (your naming tables do this well; "written for reuse" doesn't).
  • Every naming rule gets a right/wrong example. Your harmonization table is the best part of the current draft precisely because it does this. Extend that style everywhere.
  • Define failure behavior, not just success behavior. What does the AI do with a file it can't classify? A transcript in German? A 200-page PDF? A file that contradicts an existing decision? Every unhandled case becomes a silent inconsistency.
  • Prefer fewer, harder rules over many soft ones. A 12-rule constitution the AI always follows beats a 40-rule one it follows probabilistically. Every rule you add dilutes attention on the others.
  • Version it and log changes. Add a changelog section; when ingestion behavior changes, the team should be able to see why pages from March look different from pages from July.
  • Write the escape hatch explicitly. "When in doubt, do X" (your "err toward matching an existing entity" is a good example). The AI will hit doubt constantly; every ambiguous area needs a default.

Entities an ABAP team usually works with

In the vault's sense of the word — things that show up under multiple names and deserve one canonical page — an ABAP delivery team typically deals with these categories:

Organizational entities

  • Workstreams / process areas — Order-to-Cash (O2C/OTC), Procure-to-Pay (P2P/PTP), Record-to-Report (R2R), Plan-to-Produce. These are the worst alias offenders, since every client and SI abbreviates them differently.
  • Projects and releases — the program name, wave/phase names ("Wave 2", "R2 go-live"), sprint or drop identifiers.
  • People and roles — developers, functional consultants, workstream leads, the client's process owners, basis admins. Transcripts mangle names constantly.
  • Vendors, partners, and the client — often with legal-name vs. everyday-name variants.

SAP landscape entities

  • Systems and clients — SIDs like DEV/QAS/PRD plus nicknames ("the quality box", "sandbox", "the golden client"), and client numbers (100, 200).
  • Modules / functional areas — SD, MM, FI, CO, PP, WM/EWM, PM, QM. Also newer stacks: S/4HANA, Fiori, BTP.
  • Transport routes and landscapes — transport requests, the DEV→QAS→PRD path.

Technical/code entities (the biggest volume for an ABAP team)

  • WRICEF objects — the classic inventory: Workflows, Reports, Interfaces, Conversions, Enhancements, Forms, each usually with an ID like "E-001" or "RICEF-042".
  • Repository objects — programs, function modules, classes, methods, includes, packages.
  • Data dictionary objects — tables (VBAK, EKKO, BSEG…), structures, data elements, domains, CDS views.
  • Integration artifacts — IDocs (message types, basic types), BAPIs, RFCs, OData services, proxies, PI/CPI iFlows.
  • Enhancement points — user exits, BAdIs, enhancement spots, implicit enhancements.
  • Forms and outputs — SmartForms, Adobe Forms, SAPscript.

Process/document entities

  • Change requests and defects — CR numbers, ticket IDs from whatever ALM tool is in use (Solution Manager, Jira, ServiceNow), which people cite inconsistently ("CR-1023" vs "the FTDS change").
  • Specs — FS/TS documents, often referenced by object ID in one place and document title in another.

Where to start: the highest-payoff entities to nail down first in meta/entities.md are workstreams, system SIDs/nicknames, people, and WRICEF object IDs — they appear in nearly every document and have the most alias drift. Table names and standard SAP objects (VBAK, BAPI names) are lower risk: people rarely invent nicknames for them, so the technical name is the canonical name.

Email template — asking the team for their entities

Hi team,

To get started, let's begin with a simple, high-impact step: defining the core technical entities. We need to map out the systems, SAP modules, and development objects our wiki will cover, along with any alternate names they go by. Aligning these terms ensures the AI recognizes when different names refer to the same thing (for example, "QAS" in one document and "quality box" in another). This allows the AI to file everything onto a single page and prevents duplicate clutter.

Could you share the following details?

Development Standards & Tracking

  1. Naming Conventions: Is there a standard document for ABAP coding and object naming conventions that I should review?
  2. WRICEF & Transports: How are development objects and transports named or numbered today (e.g., RICEF-042), and where is the master tracker kept?
  3. Tickets & Specs: What tools are you using to track Change Requests (CRs), defects, and Technical Specs (TS)?

Landscape & Environments

  1. Systems: What are the relevant SIDs (DEV/QAS/PRD) and specific client numbers we develop and test in?
  2. SAP Modules: Which main modules (like SD, MM, FI) does the team build for most frequently?

If it's easier, we can also discuss these in a call next week. I'd also be happy to sit down with someone from your team and work through this.
